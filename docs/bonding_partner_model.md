# A learned partner model versus the online linear identifier

Date: 2026-10-26. Protocol `configs/bonding_partner_model.json` (frozen 02:58 UTC,
four dated amendments before any test-split label was used; see "Protocol
history"). Runner `regesh/bonding/train_partner_model.py`, module
`regesh/bonding/partner_model.py`, tests `tests/test_bonding_partner_model.py`,
outputs with hashes in `results/bonding/partner_model/`. Runtime 247 s on four
CPU cores, of which 219 s training. Nothing here is a causal result about an
agent's expression; see "The causal caveat".

**Headline.** On 3,040 held-out DailyDialog exchanges (1,000 dialogues), a
windowed MLP over utterance embeddings and scalar context predicts the
partner's next valence with RMSE .242 [.230, .253], better than the platform's
cold-started identifier (.275; paired difference -.033 [-.044, -.023]) and than
persistence (.266; -.024 [-.034, -.015]). But the same MLP **without the
embeddings** does slightly better (.235; embeddings add +.007 [+.004, +.009]
RMSE), pooled ridge on the 13 scalar features matches it (.235), and the
identifier itself closes most of its gap when warm-started from a pooled prior
(.244): the identifier's deficit is cold start inside short dialogues, not its
model class. Embeddings alone carry almost no aggregate valence signal (RMSE
.270, balanced sign accuracy .53). No learned model predicts any rare emotion
class; persistence, which does, has by far the best macro F1 (.545 against at
most .196) and the lowest MAE (.106) because 81% of targets are neutral. The
one place the embeddings show something is the within-item preference check:
given the history, the embedding models prefer the agent turn that actually
preceded a happy next turn over a random other turn in 71-74% of items, where
the scalar models manage 61-62% and the cold-started identifier 40%; for
negative outcomes every model is at chance. That check is a weak observational
proxy and probably measures contextual coherence as much as affect.

## Framing

Mechanism 2 of the bond (`docs/bonding.md`) identifies the partner's affect
equation online with four parameters, `P_{t+1} = b + r P_t + s_neg min(A_t,0)
+ s_pos max(A_t,0)`, from two scalar sequences: the partner's expressed affect
and the agent's. `steer()` then searches the agent's expressed valence for the
value whose one-step prediction is closest to a positive target, inside a
coordination cap. The platform simulation showed the identifier's value against
a reactant simulated partner, but every partner there was generated by an
equation of the same family, and the identifier never saw text.

This study asks the empirical question the simulation could not: on real
human-human dialogues, how well does the partner's next labelled affect follow
from (i) the two scalar valences alone, as the identifier assumes, versus (ii)
the content of the last three utterances. The learned model is the
latent-space analogue of the identifier: it takes embeddings of the partner's
previous and current turns and of the agent's reply, plus the same scalar
context, and predicts the partner's next emotion class and valence.
`rank_candidates` is then the analogue of `steer()`: instead of searching a
scalar expressed valence, the host scores candidate replies by their predicted
effect on the partner's next affect.

Frame on DailyDialog: the PARTNER is speaker A (turns 1, 3, 5, ...), the AGENT
role is speaker B (turns 2, 4, 6, ...). One example per A turn t with a B reply
at t+1 and an A turn at t+2; inputs are A's turn t-1 when it exists, A's turn t
and B's turn t+1; the target is A's annotated emotion at t+2 and its valence
under the fixed map (no_emotion 0, happiness +.7, surprise +.3, anger -.7,
disgust -.6, fear -.5, sadness -.6; justification in the config).

Seed stability of the headline model (seeds 0/1/2, selected configuration

Reading. Every model that uses B's valence beats persistence and the
cold-started identifier on RMSE and sign, and loses to persistence on MAE: with
81% zero targets the MAE-optimal prediction is zero, and a model trained on
squared error predicts small non-zero values that cost MAE on the neutral
majority. The pooled bilinear fit on DailyDialog puts the agent's positive
influence (s_pos .35) on a par with the partner's own inertia (r .35) and the
negative influence lower (s_neg .12), the opposite asymmetry from the marital
data in `dyad.py`; the ridge weights agree (standardized a_valence .071,
b_valence .065). A fresh identifier with P = 100 I cannot use any of this: the
average test dialogue has three exchanges, its first update is a near-full jump
to the first observation (.3% of predictions hit the clip), and its mean final
parameters (r .24, slopes .20/.20) barely leave the prior. Warm-starting the
same four parameters from the pooled fit recovers a model as good as the
learned scalar models on RMSE (.244 vs .235) and sign.

Per-class F1 for persistence: no_emotion .905, anger .672, disgust .700, fear
.588, happiness .491, sadness .373, surprise .086. Ridge: no_emotion .911,
happiness .462, every rare class 0. All three MLP class heads collapsed onto the
majority class under the valence-selected penalty, and the declared "balanced"
rule (probability divided by the training prior) collapses onto rare classes
because the rare-class sigmoids are uncalibrated. Within one speaker's
consecutive turns the annotated emotion persists far more often than any
learned model recovers from the training subset; a model for the next emotion
*class* should start from persistence, and a learned model should be judged
against it.

## What the embeddings add

On aggregate accuracy: nothing, with a small measured cost. Adding the 3 x 32
whitened components to the scalar MLP raises RMSE by +.007 [+.004, +.009] and
MAE by +.004; a linear ridge on the same features is worse than scalar ridge by
+.009 RMSE; embeddings alone give RMSE .270 and balanced sign accuracy .525,
barely above the constant. The pilot on the valid split (five times fewer
examples) had already shown the embedding network memorizing, and the selected
penalties (.01 for the headline, .1 for embeddings-only, the strongest in the
grid) say the valid split preferred the embeddings nearly switched off.

On within-item ranking: something, and only here. `rank_candidates` was checked
on the 572 test items whose true next A emotion is non-neutral by comparing the
prediction for the actual B turn with the prediction for one randomly drawn
other B turn of the test set, same history (seed 20261027). "Correct" means the
sign of (actual - other) matches the sign of the outcome.

The scalar models see only the candidate's gold valence and act, so 17.5% of
pairs tie (counted wrong) and the rest reduce to "the actual reply was more
positive than the random one", which is false for negative outcomes. The
embedding models separate the actual turn from a random one for positive
outcomes at .65-.74 and are at chance for negative outcomes in all cases (the
intervals include .5). Two readings compete and this check cannot separate
them: the model has learned which replies precede happiness, or it has learned
that a random turn from another conversation is incoherent with the history and
that coherent exchanges in this corpus tend to end well. A host should treat
the ranking as a coherence-weighted positivity prior, not as a predicted effect.

## The causal caveat

Everything measured here is observational next-turn prediction. Speaker B in
DailyDialog is a human who chose the reply in context, so "the partner's next
emotion given B's reply" mixes the effect of the reply with everything the
reply reveals about the situation (a thank-you follows a favour; an apology
follows a complaint). A model that predicts the next emotion well from the
reply is not thereby a model of what an agent would cause by choosing that
reply. The platform's identifier has the same status in practice, since the
agent's expression is itself chosen in response to the partner. The preference
check is the weakest kind of counterfactual evidence: it asks whether the model
would have ranked the actual reply above (for good outcomes) or below (for bad
outcomes) a reply drawn at random from another conversation, not whether an
agent that followed the ranking would obtain better outcomes. The dialogues are
scripted, the labels are annotator judgements of written text, and the "agent"
is a person.

## How a host would use rank_candidates

```python
from regesh.bonding.partner_model import LearnedPartnerModel, make_history, candidate_scalars
model = LearnedPartnerModel.load("results/bonding/partner_model/partner_model.json.gz")   # digest verified
history = make_history(a_embedding=embed(partner_turn), a_prev_embedding=embed(previous_partner_turn),   # or None
                       a_prev_emotion="no_emotion", a_emotion="sadness", a_act="inform", exchange=k)
ranked = model.rank_candidates(history, embed(candidate_replies),
                               candidate_scalars=[candidate_scalars(intended_emotion, intended_act) for ...])
ranked["valence"]              # predicted next partner valence per candidate
ranked["class_probabilities"]  # 7-class distribution per candidate
ranked["ranking"]              # candidate indices, best predicted partner valence first
```

The embeddings must come from the same model and pooling as the training cache
(`regesh.bonding.embeddings.EmbeddingClient`, mean pooling); the model applies
its stored standardization and whitened projection itself. The scalar affect
inputs are gold labels in training, so a host needs an affect recognizer for
them or uses the `embeddings` feature set, which needs none (and, on these
data, has no aggregate accuracy to offer). Like `steer()`, the output is a
recommendation for the host, subject to the same coordination cap and trust
firewall as the rest of the bond: it never changes reliability, authority or
permissions. Given the results, the honest deployment recommendation is the
cheaper one: warm-start the existing identifier from a pooled prior (`theta0`
in `summary.json`) rather than add the learned model, and use persistence for
the next emotion class.

## Protocol history: what was frozen, what was amended, and why

The protocol was frozen at 02:58 UTC before any embedding was read, and then
amended four times before any test-split label or prediction was used. Each
amendment is dated in `configs/bonding_partner_model.json` with what had been
seen at the time:

1. **PCA projection (03:04).** A timing check with random inputs at full scale
   gave 7.1 s per epoch for 4,621 inputs under CPU contention with the embedding
   server, a worst case near two hours; the embedding block was projected to
   principal components. No label had been read.
2. **Balanced sign accuracy and a longer ridge grid (03:05).** Running the scalar
   baselines on the valid split showed that a constant positive guess scores .83
   plain sign accuracy because most non-neutral targets are happiness; the mean
   of the positive- and negative-target accuracies was added. Ridge had selected
   the top of its penalty grid.
3. **Whitening (03:11).** The leading components had standard deviations near 10
   and saturated 51% of the hidden tanh units at initialization (a forward pass
   of the untrained network on test-split vectors, no labels); components were
   whitened to unit variance, which brought saturation to 15%.
4. **Selection grid instead of fixed hyperparameters, and a linear embedding
   comparator (04:01).** A learning-signal pilot on two hash-halves of the valid
   split (1,414 fitting, 277 development, 1,527 held-out examples) showed the
   frozen configuration (hidden 64, lr 1e-3, l2 1e-4, 256 components)
   memorizing: training MSE .0006, development MSE .106 against .049 for a
   constant, held-out RMSE .305 against .221 (constant) and .207 (ridge on
   scalars). A diagnostic grid located the problem in capacity, not convergence
   (l2 .1 gave a near-constant predictor at development MSE .051; l2 .01 gave
   .056-.059; l2 .001 gave .084-.094; 256 components never beat 64 or 128; lr
   3e-3 converged where 1e-3 had not). Ridge on the same embedding features gave
   held-out RMSE .209-.215 against .207 for scalars alone, so at 1.4k examples
   the embeddings carried no linear valence signal. The frozen run therefore
   selects the penalty and the component count per feature set on the full
   valid split (five times more training data than the pilot) and adds
   `ridge_embeddings` to separate "embeddings" from "nonlinearity". The pilot
   also showed the declared `balanced` class rule collapsing onto rare classes
   (accuracy .03); it was kept as declared and is reported as a failure.

The test split was not read for any of these decisions except as unlabeled
geometry in amendment 3. Once the embedding server was idle, an epoch took
.04-.06 s, so the contention that motivated amendment 1 was the server, not the
network; the projection was kept because the selection grid is defined over it.

## Limitations

- Observational next-turn prediction on scripted dialogues; no causal claim, no
  agent, no user.
- 20% training subset (2,258 dialogues, 6,918 examples), set by the embedding
  budget; the valid-split pilot suggests the embedding models are data-limited,
  but ridge on the same features also failed, so more data is not known to help.
- Embeddings are mean-pooled hidden states of a small decoder-only code model,
  not a trained sentence encoder, stored in float16; a sentence-embedding model
  could carry affect signal these do not.
- 81% neutral targets: MAE favours predicting zero; class metrics are dominated
  by the majority; rare classes have 9-66 test examples each.
- Scalar affect and act features are gold corpus labels, an oracle for the
  partner's observed affect; a host would need an affect recognizer.
- Hyperparameters were selected on the valid split after a failed frozen
  configuration (documented above); seeds 1 and 2 retrain the selected
  configuration and agree with seed 0 within .005 RMSE.
- The preference check compares the actual reply with a random reply from
  another conversation, which confounds affect with topical coherence.

## Observations on existing modules (no edits made)

- `dyad.RecursiveIdentifier`: the default prior covariance P = 100 I makes the
  first update a near-complete jump to the first observation; inside dialogues
  of about three exchanges the identifier never recovers (RMSE .275, balanced
  sign accuracy .64). Warm-starting from a pooled prior with P = I gave RMSE
  .244 and .80 with the same four parameters. This is a design limitation
  under the platform's `steer_min_samples = 6` trust gate, not a bug; a smaller
  prior or a corpus-derived `theta0` is the cheapest improvement this study
  suggests.
- `mlp.MLP.fit`: passing `x_dev` without `y_dev` raises inside `loss_value`
  rather than with a clear message; not blocking.
- `embeddings.EmbeddingClient.embed`: whitespace-only texts are embedded as "."
  but cached under the original text's hash; harmless here, worth knowing.

## Reproduce

```bash
python -m regesh.bonding.train_partner_model --config configs/bonding_partner_model.json \
  --embeddings .cache/embeddings --dailydialog .cache/dailydialog/parlai --output results-new/bonding-partner-model
python -m unittest tests.test_bonding_partner_model -v
```

The embedding cache must hold the hash-selected 20% of train and the full valid
and test splits (`regesh.bonding.embeddings` with the local model); the runner
verifies the three manifests and refuses a non-empty output directory.
